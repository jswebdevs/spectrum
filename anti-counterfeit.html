What Is Counterfeiting?

Last updated 9 November 2026

A fake land record behind a long court case. A forged certificate that wins someone a job. A copied benefit card used to collect someone else's rations. Counterfeits cost money, time and trust, and they are getting easier to make with a good printer and a phone. This guide explains how fakes are made, how they are caught, and the layers of protection that stop them.

Counterfeiting in simple terms

A counterfeit is anything made to pass as genuine when it is not. For documents and cards, it comes in a few forms:

Full fakes

A document made from scratch to look like the real one, often with a copied design and stamp.

Altered originals

A genuine document with one detail changed, such as a name, date, grade or plot number.

Copies passed as originals

A colour photocopy or print of a real document, used where only the original should count.

Photos of screens

A picture of a document on a phone or monitor, sent as if it were a scan of the paper.

Altered originals are often the hardest to catch by eye, because almost everything on them is real.

How protection works: three layers

No single feature stops every fake. Good protection uses three layers together:

  • Physical features that are hard to copy: holograms, fine microprint, special inks, laser engraving and chips in cards.
  • Digital features that prove where a document came from: a digital signature, and a QR code that links to the original record.
  • Checking, by people and by software: an app or web page that confirms a document against the issuer's record, and AI that spots signs of editing.

The first layer makes fakes expensive to produce. The second and third make them easy to catch, even when they look perfect.

Where fakes cause harm in Bangladesh

  • Land records. Forged or altered Nokol, Khatian and deeds are behind many land disputes.
  • ID and benefit cards. Copied or borrowed cards let people claim support meant for others.
  • Certificates. Fake academic, professional and trade certificates are used for jobs, admissions and permits.
  • Licences and permits. Expired or forged licences are hard to spot without a way to check them on the spot.

How to check a document quickly

Before relying on an important document, ask four questions:

  • Can I check it with the issuer? A QR code or reference number that leads to the issuing office's own record is the strongest proof.
  • Is it the original? Feel for raised print, look for holograms, and tilt it in the light. Copies are flat.
  • Do the details agree? Names, dates and numbers should match across the document and any other papers.
  • Has anything been changed? Look for uneven spacing, different fonts or blurred areas around key details.

Checks by eye help, but they are not enough on their own. A fake made with care can pass all of them. That is why the issuer's record matters most.

What protection can and cannot do

Benefits

  • Fakes are caught in seconds by anyone with a phone, without calling the issuing office.
  • Altered originals show up, because the signed record does not match.
  • Copies lose their value when only a check against the record counts.
  • A clear record of who checked what, and when, for audits.

Limits to know

  • Old documents issued before protection was added still need manual checks.
  • People must actually check. A QR code helps only if someone scans it.
  • False details at the start stay false. Identity must be checked when a document is first issued.
  • Keys must be protected. If a signing key is stolen, it must be replaced quickly.

Frequently asked questions

Can a QR code be copied onto a fake?

Yes, a QR code can be copied. That is why the code must lead to the issuer's own record. If someone copies a QR code onto a fake document, the record that opens will show the real holder's details, and the mismatch is obvious.

How does AI spot a fake document?

It looks for signs a person would miss: tiny differences in fonts and spacing, edited areas, signs of a photocopy, or a photo taken of a screen. Then it compares the document with the official record where one exists.

Do we need to replace all our old documents?

No. New documents can be issued with protection from now on, while old ones are checked against your records when they are presented.

Is a hologram enough?

On its own, no. Holograms can be imitated well enough to fool most people. They work best as one layer, together with a digital signature and a way to check the record.

Can this work without internet?

Partly. Signed QR codes can be checked offline by an app that knows the issuer's public key. A full check against the latest record needs a connection.
